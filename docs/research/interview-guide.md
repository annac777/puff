# Puff — interview notes

> **Use Hyeji's shared interview guide** (in the Puff Google Doc) for the core questions, so
> all five interviews can be synthesised together. These are supplementary notes on technique
> for Anna's two sessions, not a replacement.

**Format.** 45 minutes, semi-structured, one participant at a time.
**Sample.** 5 total — Hyeji runs 3, Anna runs 2.
**Goal.** Not to validate Puff. To find out what people actually do when they work too long,
and what it costs them to come back. If Puff turns out to be the wrong answer, this is where
that should surface.

**The method.** Critical incident technique: ask about *one specific recent time*, not about
what usually happens. People narrate their habits as tidier and more reasonable than their
habits are. A specific Tuesday afternoon resists that.

---

## Before you start

- [ ] Ask permission to record, out loud, on the recording. Say it can be stopped at any point.
- [ ] Say there are no right answers and that you did not build the thing you're asking about
      *(you did — but saying otherwise invites politeness, and politeness is not data)*.
- [ ] Have a notebook for follow-ups so you're not composing questions while they talk.
- [ ] Concept description stays face-down until minute 35.

---

## 0–5 min · Warm-up

> "Tell me what you're working on these days — what does a normal working day look like?"

Listening for: whether their work lives in a browser or in desktop apps. Don't ask directly
yet; see what they volunteer.

> "Roughly how much of that is on a computer? And where do you actually sit?"

---

## 5–15 min · Walk me through a long day

> "Think about the last time you worked on one thing for a really long stretch — a few hours,
> hard to pull away from. When was that?"

Pin it to a real day before going further. Then:

> "Take me through it. What time did you start? What were you in?"
> "When did you first notice you'd been at it a while?"
> "What did you do when you noticed?"
> "Did you eat?"

Listening for: the moment they became aware, and what happened between awareness and action —
Puff's whole opportunity lives in that gap.

**If they say they just kept going:** "What would have had to happen for you to stop?"

---

## 15–30 min · The interruption *(the core of the interview)*

> "Now think about the last time you were in the middle of something and had to stop — someone
> came in, a meeting, whatever it was. When was that?"

Rebuild it completely. Don't accept a summary.

> "What were you doing in the exact moment you got pulled away?"
> "How many things did you have open?"
> "Did you do anything before you left? Write anything down?"
> "How long were you gone?"

Then the part that matters most:

> "Walk me through coming back. What's the first thing you did when you sat down?"
> "How did you work out where you'd got to?"
> "Was there anything you'd lost?"
> "How long before you were properly back in it?"

**Follow-ups worth having ready:**
- "You said you had to find it again — find it how?"
- "Did you get back to what you'd planned to do next, or did you end up doing something else?"
- "Has that happened other times, or was that one unusual?"

**Listening for:** whether a workaround exists. Anyone who leaves themselves a note, parks a
tab, or types a half-sentence to future-self has already built a worse version of Puff by hand,
and that is the strongest signal available. Ask to see it if they have one.

---

## 30–38 min · What they've already tried

> "Have you ever used anything to make yourself take breaks?"

**If yes:**
> "How did you come across it?"
> "How long did you stick with it?"
> "Are you still using it?" → **if no:** "What made you stop?" *(let the silence sit)*
> "Do you remember a specific time it annoyed you?"

**If no:**
> "Was that a decision, or just never came up?"
> "Do you know people who do?"

This is where the case for the first trial arm comes from — a timer that fires on a clock edge —
told by someone who abandoned one.

---

## 38–45 min · The concept

Only now:

> *"A small cloud in the corner of your browser. It notices when you've been working a long
> stretch, waits until your hands actually stop rather than interrupting you mid-sentence, and
> then offers to save where you are so you can step away. When you come back, it takes you back
> to the page, along with a note to yourself if you left one."*

> "What's your first reaction?"
> "Where would that not work for you?"
> "It only works for things open in a browser tab. How much of your work is that?"
> "To know when your hands stop, it counts clicks and keystrokes — not what you type. How does
> that sit with you?"

**If they say it sounds great:** do not accept it. "When's the last time it would have helped?"
If they can't produce an occasion, the enthusiasm is politeness and should be recorded as such.

**Close:**
> "Anything I should have asked and didn't?"

---

## Things that will wreck the data

| Don't | Do |
|---|---|
| "Would you use this?" | "When did you last do X?" |
| "Do you find it hard to take breaks?" | "Tell me about the last time you worked through lunch." |
| Fill a silence | Wait. Count to five. They will keep talking. |
| Defend the design when they criticise it | "Say more about that." |
| Ask about *usually* | Ask about *the last time* |
| Explain Puff early | Keep it until minute 35 |

Write up within an hour, while you still remember tone. Capture quotes verbatim — a paraphrase
loses the thing that made it worth keeping.

---

## After all five

Pull out, per participant: where their work lives, whether resumption cost showed up
concretely, any hand-built workaround, and their history with break tools. The pattern across
five is the finding. One vivid interview is an anecdote.
